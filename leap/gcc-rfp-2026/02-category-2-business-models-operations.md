# 6. Proposed Approach and Engagement Structure
*(RFP §3.1 item 6 — 3 pages)*

## The problem we are engaged to solve

A model that works at 500 beneficiaries can fail at 5,000 for reasons that are invisible at the
smaller number: a cost per unit that only looked viable because a founder was absorbing unpaid work;
a delivery system that depends on one person's judgement; a payer who reimburses on a cycle the cash
position cannot survive; a margin that inverts the moment the service reaches beyond the easy
catchment.

Our work is to find where the model breaks before the innovator scales into it — and to specify what
has to change first.

## Our operating principle

Unit economics are the backbone. Every operating question we work on — cost structure, delivery
design, pricing, staffing, digital tooling — resolves into whether a unit of service can be delivered
sustainably at the volume the innovator intends, and what has to be true for that to hold. We build
that arithmetic honestly, including the costs founders habitually leave out, and we let the numbers
set the priority order.

## How we scope an engagement

Identical rhythm to our Category 1 work, so an innovator receiving both experiences one provider, not
two: triage call within 5 business days → one-page scoping note within 3 days → structured evidence
intake → build and pressure-test → decision session → handover and close-out note to GCC.

## What we deliver, by barrier

**"We do not know what will break when we grow."**
*Scale Barrier Diagnostic.* We map the delivery chain end to end and test each link against the target
volume: capacity ceilings, cost behaviour as volume rises, dependency on named individuals, supply and
procurement constraints, payer and collection cycles, quality-control limits. Output is a ranked
barrier register — what breaks, at what volume, what it costs to fix, and what happens if it is not
fixed. Ranked by binding constraint, so the innovator works on the one that actually holds them back.

**"Our economics do not add up and we are not sure why."**
*Unit Economics and Cost Structure Rebuild.* We define the true unit, then build cost per unit from
the ground up — direct delivery cost, allocated overhead, the founder and volunteer time that is
really a cost, customer or beneficiary acquisition cost, and the cost of failed or repeated service.
We model how each behaves as volume rises: what is genuinely fixed, what is variable, what is
step-fixed and jumps at a threshold. Output is a live unit-economics model the innovator operates,
showing contribution per unit today, the break-even volume, and the specific levers that move it.

**"Our operating model was designed for a smaller organisation."**
*Operating Model Design.* Service delivery design, role and decision-rights mapping, process
standardisation where variance is expensive, make-versus-buy and partnership decisions, and the
staffing and cost plan that supports the target volume. We specify the operating model the innovator
is scaling into, and the sequence and cost to get from here to there.

**"We think technology could make us more efficient but we do not know where."**
*Digital and AI Efficiency Review.* We start from the cost and time data, not from the technology: we
identify where staff time actually goes, then assess which of those activities can responsibly be
automated or assisted, and which cannot. We are explicit about where AI should not be used —
clinical judgement, safeguarding decisions, anything requiring accountable human review, and any
workflow handling sensitive beneficiary data in tools that do not control retention. Output is a
prioritised shortlist with implementation cost, realistic time saved, risks, and an honest "do not do
this" column. We recommend leaving a process manual more often than innovators expect.

**"We have a growth plan but no costed path to it."**
*Growth Plan and Implementation Roadmap.* Growth built on demonstrated absorption capacity and
defensible unit economics rather than ambition — phased volume targets, the capability and hiring
plan behind them, the capital requirement each phase implies, and a KPI tree the leadership team can
actually run against.

## How this fits GCC's operating model

Time-bound, decision-centric, individually commissioned, invoiced against a maximum contract amount,
reported to GCC through scoping and close-out notes. Where a diagnostic reveals the binding
constraint is financial rather than operational — or the reverse — we say so and recommend the right
category, including where that means another provider.

Our close-out notes carry portfolio-level observations: where several innovators share a structural
barrier, a single Category 5 cohort session is better value to GCC than five separate engagements,
and we will say so even though it is the smaller commission.

## How we track whether it worked

| Measured | How |
|---|---|
| Barrier register produced and prioritised with the innovator | Close-out note |
| Unit-economics model operated by the innovator unaided at 90 days | Follow-up check |
| Change in contribution per unit, or in break-even volume | Model-verified against baseline |
| Decisions taken on the operating model within the window | Innovator-confirmed |
| Volume served against plan at 6 months, where scaling followed | Innovator-reported |

We record the baseline at the start of every engagement so that any claimed improvement is measured
against a number we can both point to, rather than asserted afterwards.

## What these have in common

We do not accept a cost structure as given. We rebuild it from the underlying activity, test how it
behaves at the volume the organisation actually intends to reach, and put the trade-offs in front of
the decision-maker with the consequences quantified — early enough that the decision is still open.

**Portfolio-level track record relevant to this category:** over 100 early-stage ventures advised;
50+ financial and operating models built and reviewed across software-as-a-service, e-commerce,
financial technology, climate technology and real estate; 150 projects evaluated. Our modelling
practice is the same discipline applied to operations: if the arithmetic of delivery does not work,
nothing downstream of it does.
